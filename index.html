7 Bridges of Königsberg

How a city puzzle became a mathematical concept.

SCAN · EXPLORE · UNDERSTAND

🌉 The Problem

Can you cross all seven bridges exactly once?

The old Prussian city of Königsberg was divided by the Pregel River into two islands and two mainland banks, connected by seven bridges.

“Try crossing all bridges without using a bridge twice!”

The puzzle looks like a question about walking through a city. Euler showed that the important part is not the exact map — it is the pattern of connections.

🧠 Euler's Breakthrough

Mathematician Leonhard Euler studied the seven-bridges problem and simplified the city into a mathematical network.

LANDMASSES

→ Vertices

Each land region becomes a point, or node.

BRIDGES

→ Edges

Each bridge becomes a connection, or edge.

This way of thinking helped establish the foundations of what we now call graph theory.

🔵 What Is Graph Theory?

Graph theory studies structures made from objects and the connections between them.

Vertices / Nodes

Represent locations, people, webpages, devices, or other objects.

Edges / Connections

Represent roads, relationships, links, or other connections.

For the Königsberg problem, the four land regions are represented by vertices and the seven bridges by edges.

🔢 The Key Idea: Degree

The degree of a vertex is the number of edges connected to it.

For a closed route that uses every edge exactly once, every vertex must have an even degree. The original Königsberg network does not satisfy this condition.

Therefore, the seven bridges cannot all be crossed exactly once in a closed walk.

This is the central mathematical insight behind the classic problem.

🌐 From Bridges to the Modern World

🗺️ Routes & Maps

Locations can be represented as nodes and roads as edges to study routes through a network.

🌐 The Internet

Webpages and their links can be represented as a network.

👥 Social Networks

People can be nodes and relationships can be edges.

⚙️ Network Analysis

Graph-based methods help us understand and optimize connected systems.

💡 The Big Idea

Euler changed the question.

Instead of asking only “How do I physically cross these bridges?”, we can ask:

“What is the structure of the connections?”

That shift from focusing on the physical layout to focusing on connectivity is the powerful idea behind graph theory.
